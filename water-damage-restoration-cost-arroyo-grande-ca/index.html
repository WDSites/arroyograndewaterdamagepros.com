Why there is no honest flat price

Anyone who quotes a water damage restoration cost over the phone before seeing the room is guessing. A leaking dishwasher line that soaked one cabinet base and a failed water heater that flooded a hallway can both be called water damage, yet they require different crews, different equipment and different days on site.

What you can get is a written scope. A scope lists the work in order, the materials affected and the equipment that will run. Price follows from it. If a company cannot show you a scope, you cannot compare it to anything.

This post covers how a scope gets built, what moves the total up or down and the questions that expose a thin quote. It does not give dollar figures, because no responsible company can without a site visit.

How a scope is built, in the order it happens

  1. 1

    Source and category

    The tech identifies where the water came from and sorts it into Category 1, 2 or 3 under ANSI/IICRC S500. Dirtier water means more removal and more cleaning.

  2. 2

    Moisture mapping

    Moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera show how far the water traveled. The map decides which walls, floors and ceilings need work.

  3. 3

    Extraction and demolition

    Standing water is pumped out. Materials that cannot be dried, such as carpet pad or soaked insulation, are tagged for removal.

  4. 4

    Drying plan

    The crew picks the number of air movers and LGR dehumidifiers for the cubic footage of the affected rooms, plus the days they will run.

  5. 5

    Monitoring

    Readings are taken on each visit and logged. Equipment comes out only when materials reach their dry standard.

  6. 6

    Rebuild

    Drywall, insulation, flooring, paint and trim are replaced after drying. Many companies price this separately or hand it to another contractor.

Arroyo Grande note: check the license before you sign

California requires a contractor license for work of $1,000 or more in combined labor and materials. That threshold rose from $500 to $1,000 on January 1, 2025 under AB 2622. A water loss can pass $1,000 quickly once a drying system and a wall repair are included, so assume the rule applies to you.

The exemption also does not apply if the work needs a permit or the person hires workers. Before a crew starts at your Arroyo Grande home, look the license up on the Contractors State License Board website and match the name and number on the quote to the record. If a company will not give you a license number in writing, walk away.

Comparing two written scopes side by side

Lay both quotes on the table and mark every line item that appears in one and not the other. A missing line is not always a trick, but it is always a question. One company may include a thermal imaging scan while another assumes the tech will just feel the wall.

Check that the equipment count and day count are written, not implied. A quote that says drying equipment as one line with no numbers can quietly grow once the job is underway. Ask also whether monitoring visits are included or billed each time.

If you are weighing water damage cleanup against a bigger project that also includes water damage repair, ask each company to price the two phases separately so you can see where the dollars sit.

What a vague quote usually hides

No moisture readings

Without baseline numbers, nobody can prove the structure is dry at the end.

Lump-sum drying

One figure with no equipment list makes it impossible to compare or challenge.

Rebuild left out

The low drying price looks attractive until the drywall, paint and flooring arrive as a second bill.

No mention of category

If dirty water is treated like clean water, the job can get redone.

Open-ended add-ons

Phrases like as needed or additional items may apply deserve a written limit.

Before you call anyone

A few steps protect both your home and your ability to compare quotes later.

  • Shut off the main water valve or the fixture valve at the source.
  • Photograph and video all damage, with a wide shot and a close shot of each wet area.
  • Write down when you first noticed the water and what you did about it.
  • Ask for the license number and proof of insurance before work starts.
  • Request a scope in writing with equipment counts, planned drying days and what is excluded.
  • Keep damaged items until your insurer says you can discard them.
  • Ask whether the crew follows ANSI/IICRC S500.

Questions Homeowners Ask

Does the price change if water damage happens at night?

It can. Some companies list an after-hours line item, others do not. Ask for it to be shown separately so you can compare it fairly with a daytime quote.

Why are two quotes for the same room so far apart?

Most often the cheaper one leaves out something, such as monitoring, a drying day count or the rebuild. Put the two scopes side by side and look for missing lines before you assume either company is padding.

Is the cheapest scope always a bad sign?

No, but it deserves a closer look. A low number with clear equipment counts and written readings is fine. A low number with no detail is a risk.

Do I pay for drying and rebuilding at the same time?

Not necessarily. Drying comes first and the rebuild follows once readings are acceptable. Ask whether both phases are in one scope or two, and how each is invoiced.

Will my homeowners insurance pay for restoration?

It depends on how the water got in. Sudden and accidental damage is often covered, gradual leaks often are not, and flood usually needs separate coverage. Check your policy and ask your insurer before you decide.

How can I tell if a contractor is licensed for this kind of work in California?

Look up the name and license number on the Contractors State License Board website. Since January 1, 2025, work of $1,000 or more in combined labor and materials generally requires a license. Confirm the license is active and matches the business name on your quote.
